
## Systems Thinking Philosophy

### 1. What is Systems Thinking?

Systems Thinking is an analytical and conceptual approach that views
phenomena as *interconnected wholes* rather than isolated parts.
It emphasises:
* Relationships over components
* Dynamics over static structures
* Patterns over events
* Feedback over linear causality

The central premise is: Behaviour emerges from the structure and
interactions within a system, not merely from the properties of
individual elements.

### 2. Historical Roots

#### General Systems Theory

The foundations of Systems Thinking are strongly associated with
*Ludwig von Bertalanffy*, who introduced:
* *General Systems Theory (GST)*
GST challenged reductionism by arguing that many phenomena require
holistic analysis, especially where interactions and organisation
shape outcomes.


#### Cybernetics & Feedback

Developments in cybernetics, including feedback and control theory,
influenced Systems Thinking by highlighting:
* Self-regulation
* Feedback loops
* Stability and adaptation


#### System Dynamics

*Jay Forrester* advanced the field through:
* System Dynamics
* Modelling feedback-driven behaviour over time

Applications included:
* Industrial systems
* Urban systems
* Environmental systems


#### Organisational & Social Systems

Systems Thinking entered management and organisational theory largely through:
* *Peter Senge*

Notably:
* *The Fifth Discipline*


#### Sustainability & Ecology

*Donella Meadows* popularised Systems Thinking for broader audiences:
* *Thinking in Systems*

Her work emphasised leverage points, unintended consequences, and sustainability.


### 3. Purpose of Systems Thinking

Systems Thinking aims to:
1. Understand *complex, interconnected problems*
2. Reveal *hidden structures and feedback loops*
3. Identify *leverage points for intervention*
4. Avoid *unintended consequences*

It is particularly valuable where linear models fail.


### 4. Why Use Systems Thinking?

#### Key Motivations

* Many problems are *systemic*, not isolated
* Local optimisation can worsen global outcomes
* Cause–effect chains are often nonlinear
* Delays and feedback distort intuition


#### Typical Problems Addressed

* Sustainability challenges
* Organisational dysfunction
* Policy resistance
* Technological ecosystems
* Socio-technical systems


### 5. How Does Systems Thinking Work?

#### 1. Define System Boundaries

* What is inside the system?
* What is external but influential?

Example: A computing system includes hardware,
software, users, energy supply, lifecycle impacts.


#### 2. Identify Elements

* Components
* Actors
* Resources


#### 3. Map Relationships

* Dependencies
* Flows (information, energy, materials)
* Constraints

Tools:
* Causal loop diagrams
* Stock-and-flow models
* Influence maps


#### 4. Analyse Feedback Loops

*Reinforcing loops*
-> Amplify change (growth, collapse)

*Balancing loops*
-> Stabilise systems (control, regulation)


#### 5. Consider Dynamics Over Time

* Delays
* Accumulation effects
* Oscillations
* Nonlinear transitions


#### 6. Identify Leverage Points

Places where small interventions produce large effects:
* Rules
* Information flows
* System goals
* Feedback structures


#### 7. Evaluate Interventions

* Short-term vs long-term effects
* Trade-offs
* Side effects


### 6. When Should Systems Thinking Be Used?

#### Appropriate Contexts

* Complex, multi-factor problems
* Situations with feedback and delays
* Interdisciplinary research
* Sustainability and policy analysis
* Socio-technical systems


#### Less Suitable When

* Problems are purely mechanical and linear
* Interactions are negligible
* System boundaries are trivial


### 7. Core Philosophical Assumptions

1. *Wholes exhibit emergent properties*
2. *Structure drives behaviour*
3. *Causality is often circular*
4. *Feedback governs stability and change*
5. *Optimisation must consider the whole system*


### 8. Systems Thinking vs Reductionism

| Reductionist View | Systems Thinking View  |
|-------------------|------------------------|
| Analyse parts     | Analyse relationships  |
| Linear causality  | Feedback loops         |
| Static structure  | Dynamic behaviour      |
| Local solutions   | System-wide effects    |



### 9. Typical Outcomes

* Deeper understanding of complexity
* Identification of unintended consequences
* Recognition of systemic risks
* Improved intervention strategies
* Holistic design insights


### 10. Relevance to Sustainable Computing

Systems Thinking is highly applicable because
sustainable computing involves:
* Energy flows
* Hardware/software interaction
* Lifecycle impacts
* User behaviour
* Infrastructure dependencies


#### Example Applications

*1. Energy Efficiency*
Avoid shifting consumption rather than reducing it.

*2. Distributed Architectures*
Understand trade-offs between:

* Local efficiency
* Network overhead
* Reliability

*3. Lifecycle Sustainability*

Consider:
* Manufacturing
* Operation
* Maintenance
* Disposal


### 11. Complementarity with Design Science Research

* *Systems Thinking* -> Understand complexity & structure
* *DSR* -> Build & evaluate artefacts

Together:
1. Systems Thinking informs problem framing and design choices
2. DSR produces and tests concrete solutions
