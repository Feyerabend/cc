
## Teaching / Learning

In a classroom working through the second chapter on virtual machines, the material can
initially be presented in a traditional teacher–student format, with a lecture used to
establish the core ideas and terminology. This introductory framing is useful, but the
primary emphasis remains on project-based learning, where students actively design,
implement, and test components of their own virtual machines. The classroom activities
should therefore shift quickly from exposition to hands-on exploration, guided experimentation,
and structured exercises.

Large language models may be introduced as supportive tools--for example, to clarify concepts,
illustrate alternative designs, or provide quick feedback on student questions—but they
should not be relied upon extensively, at this time. The main learning outcomes are best
achieved when students engage directly with the material through their own implementations
and problem-solving efforts.

Some ideas on how to proceed can be found in [PLAN.md](./PLAN.md).

Teacher focus:
- Stacks, heaps, bytecode, interpretation vs compilation
- VM as a contract, not magic

Student tasks:
- Trace execution manually
- Map high-level constructs to VM-level behaviour

### Example: VM as Contract

When students first encounter a virtual machine or runtime, they often treat it as magic.
The program runs, the language works, and the mechanisms in between are ignored. This
attitude is often reinforced by teaching that says “the runtime handles it” or “the VM
takes care of execution.” While convenient, this framing obscures what is actually being
learned.

In a teaching setting, a virtual machine should not be presented as a complete model of
real-world systems. It is better understood as a *deliberately limited abstraction*:
a simplified execution model that makes certain rules explicit while omitting others.
Its value lies not in realism, but in clarity.

A VM, even a minimal one, defines a contract. That contract specifies what the language
*guarantees within this model* and what is intentionally left outside it. Every program
runs according to those rules, and behaviour that falls outside them is either undefined
in the model or simply not represented at all. This is not a flaw; it is what allows the
VM to be small enough to reason about.

In this sense, the VM does not explain everything about execution, memory, or performance.
Instead, it explains *just enough* to support specific concepts: sequencing, state,
control flow, and the relationship between instructions and data. When students understand
that the VM is a contract at this level, surprising behaviour stops looking arbitrary and
starts looking like a consequence of the rules they have been shown.

This framing also helps address an important misconception: that one piece of source code
has a single, intrinsic behaviour. Even within a teaching VM, small changes to assumptions
can produce different outcomes. This mirrors real systems conceptually, without claiming
to model all their complexity. The lesson is not that "anything can happen," but that
behaviour is always relative to a specified execution model.

A productive teaching approach is therefore to make the abstraction explicit. Take a small
program and trace its execution according to the VM's rules: show the instruction stream,
the program state, and the points where the model defines what happens next. Then adjust
one aspect of the model, such as how memory is represented or how instructions are stepped,
and observe what changes and what remains invariant. This reveals which properties come
from the contract and which were accidental.

It is equally important to show the edges of the model. Some behaviours are simply not
defined in this VM, not because they are forbidden in all systems, but because they lie
outside the scope of what the abstraction promises. Emphasise that when students rely on
a property, they should be able to point to where it is guaranteed by the model, not to
intuition or habit.

The goal of teaching VMs this way is not to replace real runtimes, but to train a habit of
mind. Students should move from "the runtime will handle it" to "what does this model
actually guarantee?" That habit scales naturally to real compilers, production runtimes,
and even to reasoning about code generated by tools or language models.
