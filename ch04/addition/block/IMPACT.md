
## Impact: Why This Work Matters

### Technology Is Really Never Neutral

When you write code, you might feel like you're just solving technical
puzzles--making functions work, fixing bugs, optimising algorithms.
But every technical decision you make carries human consequences.

Consider the blockchain projects here. When you decide:
- *How long blocks should be stored* -> You're deciding how long records follow people
- *Who can read the chain* -> You're deciding who has access to potentially sensitive information
- *What consensus mechanism to use* -> You're deciding who gets to participate and at what cost
- *Whether data can be deleted* -> You're deciding whether people can escape their past

These aren't just technical specifications. They're choices about power,
privacy, memory, and access. You're not just building systems--you're shaping
the rules that will govern how people interact, transact, and trust each other.


### The Wide Dimensions of Impact

Blockchain doesn't exist in isolation. It connects to:

*Economic Systems*: When you design a cryptocurrency or token system, you're creating
rules about wealth distribution. Who can mine? Who gets rewarded? What can be bought
and sold? These choices affect who prospers and who struggles. Real people stake their
savings, their livelihoods, their futures on these systems.

*Legal and Political Structures*: Blockchain often promises to replace or bypass existing
institutions--banks, governments, legal systems. But these institutions, however flawed,
provide consumer protections, dispute resolution, democratic accountability, and social
safety nets. When you build systems that circumvent them, you need to ask: what are we
losing? Who is vulnerable without these protections?

*Social Relationships*: How we exchange value shapes how we relate to each other. When
every interaction becomes a transaction recorded forever, when trust is replaced by
cryptographic proof, when communities are governed by immutable code--we change the
nature of human connection. We may gain efficiency but lose forgiveness, gain transparency
but lose privacy, gain automation but lose human judgment.

*Environmental Systems*: The energy consumption of Proof of Work isn't an abstract problem.
It's coal burned, rivers warmed, communities displaced by mining operations. Every hash
you compute has a physical footprint. The carbon emissions from cryptocurrency mining
contribute to climate change that disproportionately harms people who have benefited
least from the technology.

*Cultural Values*: What we choose to build reflects what we value. If we build systems
that reward speculation over creation, that prioritize financial gain over community
wellbeing, that make everything ownable and tradeable--we're making a statement about
what matters. We're teaching people that everything has a price, that value means
profit, that relationships are transactions.


### Integration Into Society, Not Standalone

No technology operates in a vacuum. Blockchain isn't just a clever data structure--it
becomes embedded in:

*Existing Inequalities*: Blockchain may promise decentralization, but it enters a world
of profound inequality. Access to technology, financial literacy, stable internet, and
surplus capital to invest--these aren't evenly distributed. A "permissionless" system
that requires expensive hardware to participate, or technical knowledge most people
don't have, or disposable income to weather volatility--this isn't actually accessible
to everyone.

*Regulatory Frameworks*: Governments and institutions will respond to blockchain technology.
They'll create laws, impose regulations, build enforcement mechanisms. Your technical
choices today shape tomorrow's policies. They influence whether regulations protect
innovation or stifle it, whether they shield the powerful or protect the vulnerable.

*Social Infrastructure*: Blockchain doesn't replace the need for education, healthcare,
housing, community support. If cryptocurrency speculation diverts resources from these
essentials, if NFT marketplaces receive more funding than public services, if "Web3"
attention eclipses urgent social needs--that's a choice we're making collectively, and
one you participate in by choosing what to build.

*Historical Context*: Technology has repeatedly promised liberation while creating new
forms of control. The printing press, the internet, social media--each brought genuine
benefits and unexpected harms. Blockchain is part of this history. Understanding past
technologies' impacts helps you anticipate your own work's consequences.


### This Is Important. It Can Change Lives.

Your work as a technologist has real power. The systems you build will:

*Provide opportunity or create barriers*: A well-designed digital identity system could
help refugees prove their credentials and rebuild their lives. A poorly designed one
could create a permanent underclass locked out by algorithmic decisions they can't appeal.

*Enable autonomy or enforce control*: Smart contracts could free people from exploitative
intermediaries. They could also trap people in agreements they can't escape, enforced by
unstoppable code with no mechanism for mercy or context.

*Build wealth or extract it*: Cryptocurrency has made some people rich. It has also enabled
scams that have devastated families, particularly in communities targeted by predatory marketing.

*Foster connection or enable harm*: Decentralized platforms could create communities free
from corporate control. They could also create spaces beyond legal accountability where
harassment, exploitation, and illegal activity flourish.


### As Other Professions Also Do

You're not alone in having this responsibility. Consider:

*Doctors* choose treatments that could heal or harm. They take Hippocratic oaths to
"first, do no harm" and are held accountable by professional standards, ethics boards,
and legal systems.

*Architects* design spaces that could enable community or enforce segregation. A poorly
designed building can trap people in fires. A thoughtfully designed public space can
bring diverse people together.

*Teachers* shape minds and futures. What they teach, how they teach it, who they
encourage or discourage--these choices echo through generations.

*Journalists* decide which stories to tell and how to frame them. They can illuminate
injustice or perpetuate it, depending on whose voices they amplify and whose they ignore.


As a technologist, you have similar power and similar responsibility. But unlike many
professions, software development often lacks:
- *Professional standards* that enforce ethical practice
- *Licensing requirements* that ensure baseline competence and accountability  
- *Institutional oversight* that can intervene when harm occurs
- *Cultural norms* that prioritize human welfare over technical achievement

This means you must hold yourself accountable. You must develop your own ethical framework.
You must ask difficult questions even when no one requires you to.


### Your Responsibility

When you build blockchain systems--or any technology--you're not just an engineer.
You're:

*An architect of social systems*: Your code becomes the rules by which people interact.
Design with intention. Consider who you're designing for and who might be harmed.

*A steward of power*: The systems you build will concentrate or distribute power.
Be conscious of this. Ask who benefits from your choices and who bears the costs.

*A participant in democracy*: Technology shapes what's possible in society.
You're helping decide what kind of future we build together. Engage thoughtfully
with the political dimensions of your work.

*A member of a community*: You're not building for abstract "users"--you're building
for real people with hopes, fears, families, and struggles.
Center their humanity in your work.

*A learner*: You won't always get it right. Systems will have unintended consequences.
Stay curious, stay humble, stay willing to listen--especially to those harmed by your work.


### Questions to Carry Forward

As you work on these projects and throughout your career, keep asking:
- *Who is missing from this conversation?* Whose voices aren't at the table when decisions are made?
- *What am I taking for granted?* What assumptions am I making about access, knowledge, resources, context?
- *Who benefits? Who pays?* Follow the incentives. Trace the consequences beyond the immediate users.
- *What are the second-order effects?* A system might work well at small scale but create problems
  at large scale. What happens when your prototype becomes infrastructure?
- *Can this be undone?* What mechanisms exist for correction, appeal, or change? What happens when you're wrong?
- *Is this making the world I want to live in?* Does this project align with your values?
  Would you want this system applied to you, your family, your community?


### The Choice Is Yours

Every line of code you write is a choice. Every system you build is a vote for a particular kind of future.
You can choose to:
- *Build extractive systems* that concentrate wealth and power, or *distributive systems* that spread opportunity
- *Create surveillance infrastructure* that tracks and controls, or *privacy-respecting tools* that protect autonomy
- *Automate away human judgment* entirely, or *augment human capability* while preserving agency
- *Move fast and break things* (often breaking people in the process), or *move thoughtfully* with those affected
- *Build for the privileged* who already have resources, or *build for those* who are currently excluded
- *Follow incentives* blindly toward profit, or *question incentives* and build toward genuine wellbeing

This work matters. The choices you make will ripple outward, affecting lives in ways you may never fully see.
That's both a burden and an extraordinary opportunity.

*Take it seriously. Question constantly. Listen deeply. Build wisely.*
