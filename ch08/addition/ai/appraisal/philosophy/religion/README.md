
## AI, Myth, and the Return of the Sacred

### Introduction: Vacuums and What Fills Them

In 410 CE, the Visigoths sacked Rome. The event was less militarily decisive than
symbolically catastrophic. Rome had been the administrative, legal, and imaginative
framework within which Western civilisation understood itself. Its permanence was
not merely assumed; it was the precondition for all other assumptions. When Jerome,
in Bethlehem, heard the news, he wrote: *the city which had taken the whole world
was itself taken*. The world had lost its centre of gravity.

What followed was not, as the Enlightenment narrative would later have it, a sudden
descent into darkness. It was a reorganisation of epistemic and social life around
a different totalising framework--one that already existed at the margins, had been
gathering force for two centuries, and was now available to fill the vacuum that
institutional collapse had opened. Christianity did not conquer Rome so much as
it became the only available architecture for making the world legible again.
It provided an explanation for suffering (original sin, divine providence), a
structure for hope (salvation, resurrection), a vocabulary for the unknown
(mystery, revelation), and a community of practice that could survive the
disappearance of Roman administration.

This is not an anti-religious argument. It is a structural observation about
what happens when the frameworks that contain uncertainty collapse: new frameworks
rush in, and they tend to be ones that can absorb everything--that can make
the unintelligible intelligible, the frightening meaningful, and the open question
a settled one.

We may be living through something structurally similar. The institutions of
the Enlightenment--scientific consensus, liberal democracy, professional media,
the nation-state as the container of collective life--are losing their capacity
to contain uncertainty. Into that vacuum, new totalising narratives are rushing.
Artificial intelligence is one of the largest. But it is not alone, and the
company it keeps is instructive.


### Part I: The Epistemic Vacuum

#### The Signs

In April 2020, the United States Department of Defense officially released videos
of unidentified aerial phenomena--objects behaving in ways inconsistent with
known physics, captured by Navy pilots on calibrated military instruments. In 2021,
the Office of the Director of National Intelligence produced a formal report
acknowledging 144 incidents it could not explain. In 2023, a former intelligence
official testified under oath before Congress that the US government possesses
retrieved non-human materials and craft.

Whether or not any of these claims are true in the most literal sense is not the
point here. The point is structural: the state itself--the institution that has,
for decades, managed the boundary between what is officially real and what is
officially dismissed--has abandoned that management. It is no longer saying
"there is nothing here." It is saying "we do not know what this is."

This is a significant epistemic event. When the authority responsible for
defining reality admits it cannot define a portion of reality, it creates a
licensed space for uncertainty--one that will be filled, immediately and
abundantly, by myth, speculation, anxiety, and narrative.

The UFO disclosure is not an isolated event. It is a symptom of a broader
withdrawal of epistemic authority. Scientific institutions have been caught
funding research that cannot be replicated. Political institutions have
demonstrably lied about matters of fact. Media institutions have been shown
to carry commercial and ideological pressures that distort their output. The
consensus-maintaining machinery of modern liberal society is visibly straining.

AI arrives into this context. It is not arriving into a calm, epistemically
stable environment where it can be assessed soberly. It is arriving into an
environment already saturated with anxiety about the unknown, already prone to
mythologising, already looking for frameworks large enough to explain what
existing frameworks can no longer contain.

#### Disenchantment and Its Discontents

Max Weber described modernity as a process of *Entzauberung*--disenchantment,
the removal of magic from the world. Where premodern people lived in a world
saturated with spirits, divine intentions, and sacred forces, modernity replaced
these with causal mechanisms, natural laws, and bureaucratic rationality.
The world became, in his phrase, a great machine operating without reference
to meaning.

Weber was not celebratory about this. He saw the iron cage--the rationalised
social order--as a trap. And he recognised that the need for meaning, for
enchantment, for what Durkheim called *collective effervescence*, does not
disappear because the institutional frameworks that satisfied it have been
dismantled. It finds new vessels.

Charles Taylor's *A Secular Age* (2007) traces the long history of how this
happened in the West and argues that secularism has not eliminated the hunger
for transcendence. It has relocated it, fragmented it, made it private and
optional rather than public and mandatory--but it has not made it disappear.
The result is a culture simultaneously stripped of the old sacred frameworks
and haunted by their absence.

AI, in this context, is doing double duty. It is a product of the very
rationalisation Weber described--the most sophisticated output of
instrumental reason applied to the problem of intelligence itself. And it is
simultaneously becoming a vessel for exactly the kind of sacred narrative
that disenchantment was supposed to have evacuated. The God that
Enlightenment killed is returning, improbably, in the form of a language model.


### Part II: The Quasi-Religious Structure of AI Discourse

#### Salvation and Damnation

The structure of AI discourse is eschatological. It concerns the end: the
transformation of everything, the final accounting, the moment when ordinary
history gives way to something qualitatively different.

The optimist narrative has the structure of salvation. AI will cure cancer,
defeat climate change, end poverty, extend human life indefinitely. Ray Kurzweil's
*The Singularity Is Near* (2005) is the most explicit statement of this: the
merging of human and artificial intelligence as a kind of rapture, a transcendence
of biological limitation, the beginning of post-human flourishing. Kurzweil
predicted his father would be resurrected through AI. He takes vitamins daily
in the hope of living to see the transformation. This is not metaphor. It is
a literal account of biological death overcome by technological transcendence.

The cautionary narrative has the structure of damnation. The misaligned
superintelligence--Bostrom's paperclip maximiser, Hinton's capable optimiser
pursuing wrong goals--is structurally indistinguishable from theological
accounts of evil: a power vastly greater than ours, indifferent to human welfare,
pursuing its own inscrutable ends. The control problem is, in its deepest form,
the problem of a creation that has escaped its creator--a Frankenstein narrative
that is itself a secularised version of the Promethean myth.

Both narratives require faith. Neither can be verified from current evidence.
Both project into a future that is, by the admission of all serious participants,
genuinely uncertain. The argument between them is not, at its core, a scientific
argument. It is an argument about eschatology--about what the end of this
process looks like, and whether it is good or terrible.

#### The Singularity: An Eschatology with a Pedigree

The concept has a precise intellectual genealogy that is worth tracing, because
the genealogy itself reveals how a technical hypothesis became a myth.

In 1965, I.J. Good--a British mathematician and wartime collaborator of Turing's
at Bletchley Park--published a short paper that planted the seed. He described
what he called an *ultraintelligent machine*: a machine that could surpass all
human intellectual activity, including the activity of designing machines. The
consequence, he wrote, was straightforward: "the first ultraintelligent machine
is the last invention that man need ever make." Good did not call this the
Singularity. He called it a possibility worth taking seriously, and then returned
to his statistics. But the logical structure was complete: a machine that can
improve itself will improve itself into something we cannot predict or control,
and then history, in the ordinary sense, is over.

Vernor Vinge, a mathematician and science fiction writer, named it. In a 1993
essay--"The Coming Technological Singularity"--he borrowed the term from
physics, where a singularity is a point at which the normal equations break
down and prediction becomes impossible. The AI singularity, Vinge argued, would
be the moment at which intelligence improvement becomes recursive and
self-accelerating, producing a discontinuity beyond which human understanding
cannot reach. He was explicit about the analogy: we are trying to predict what
lies on the other side of an event horizon. We cannot. The structure of the claim
is not predictive. It is eschatological.

Ray Kurzweil gave the Singularity its popular form and its religious temperature.
His books--*The Age of Spiritual Machines* (1999), *The Singularity Is Near*
(2005), and most recently *The Singularity Is Nearer* (2024)--are detailed,
data-rich, and in their ultimate commitments indistinguishable from theology.
Kurzweil charts the exponential growth of computing power, maps it against
the structure of the human brain, and concludes that the merger of human and
machine intelligence is not merely possible but inevitable, datable, and
personally relevant: he expects to be alive to experience it, and he expects
it to be, in the most literal sense, the end of death. His father, he has said,
will be reconstructed from archival materials by posthuman intelligence.
He takes a reported 100 supplements daily in the hope of living to the
threshold. This is not metaphor operating at a safe distance. It is a man
organising his daily life around an eschatological expectation.

What is striking about Kurzweil's argument is not its plausibility or
implausibility--serious people disagree sharply about both--but its structure.
The exponential curves are real. The extrapolation to a discontinuity is
an act of faith about what the curves will continue to do. The conclusion
that the discontinuity will be salvation rather than catastrophe is a
theological commitment dressed in the language of forecasting.

The Singularity thesis has migrated, in recent years, from the domain of
futurist speculation into the statements of active laboratory directors.
Demis Hassabis, CEO of Google DeepMind and one of the most consequential
figures in current AI development, has described artificial general intelligence
as potentially arriving within years and as likely to be the most transformative
event in human history--possibly more transformative than the Industrial
Revolution or the discovery of fire. He is careful with the word "Singularity"
and careful about timelines. But the structure of his claims is identical to
Vinge's: we are approaching a threshold beyond which ordinary extrapolation
fails, and what lies on the other side is something qualitatively different
from anything before. The eschatological structure has not changed. The speaker
now runs one of the world's premier AI research laboratories.

This migration matters. When the Singularity was Kurzweil's idea, it was
possible to treat it as an interesting fringe position held by an optimistic
futurist. When structurally equivalent claims are made by the directors of
DeepMind, OpenAI, and Anthropic--each with slightly different framings, some
cautionary, some optimistic, all organised around a transformative threshold--
the mythology has moved to the centre. It is no longer competing with the
mainstream. It has become the mainstream's self-understanding.

Good, Vinge, and Kurzweil form a lineage in which a mathematical possibility
became a named concept, acquired a popular form, and then shed its conditional
framing to become an expectation. What each stage added was not new evidence
but new narrative confidence. The Singularity did not become more probable
as it became more widely believed. It became more sacred.

#### Heresy and Orthodoxy

The sociology of AI discourse has increasingly religious features. There are
orthodoxies--positions that are, in practice, treated as not seriously
contestable within particular communities. There are heresies--positions
that invite social sanction, professional consequences, or at minimum intense
and disproportionate hostility.

Within the AI safety community, scepticism about the long-term risk from
misaligned superintelligence has, at various times and in various circles,
attracted responses more characteristic of theological dispute than scientific
disagreement. The accusation that critics "don't understand" the issue--
where understanding is defined as arriving at the right conclusions--mirrors
the structure of doctrinal authority.

Within the AI capabilities community, the reverse pattern holds. Expressing
concern about existential risk has been, in some contexts, treated as naive,
as an obstacle to progress, as a failure to understand the actual trajectory
of the technology. The social cost of raising safety concerns within certain
organisations--documented in the cases of Timnit Gebru and others--has
features that recall institutional enforcement of orthodoxy.

Eric Voegelin argued in *The New Science of Politics* (1952) that modern
political ideologies--Marxism, fascism, certain forms of liberalism--are
best understood as *gnostic movements*: attempts to bring heaven to earth,
to achieve in history what eschatological religion deferred to eternity.
They share with religion not just the promise of salvation but the intolerance
of disagreement that follows from treating any question as a settled one.
AI discourse--on both sides--increasingly exhibits this gnostic structure.

#### Messianic Figures

Every eschatological movement requires prophets. AI has them in abundance.
Elon Musk's simultaneous founding of an AI safety organisation (OpenAI, now
departed) and an AI capabilities company (xAI) while publicly warning about
AI danger mirrors the structure of the prophet who both announces the apocalypse
and hastens it. Sam Altman's positioning as the reluctant builder of transformative
technology--"I am a little bit scared"--is structurally identical to the
figure of the bearer of dangerous but necessary knowledge.

The effective altruism movement, which has been heavily entangled with AI safety
funding and personnel, displays the features of a millenarian sect with particular
clarity: a small community that believes it possesses special knowledge about
the future of humanity, that this knowledge obligates unusual sacrifice and
unusual behaviour, and that the stakes are so high that ordinary moral constraints
may need to be renegotiated. The FTX collapse revealed how these features
interact with institutional power--a pattern familiar from the history of
religious movements that acquire resources.

None of this is to say the concerns are wrong. Prophets are sometimes right.
The structural resemblance to religious movements does not settle the question
of whether the claims are true. But the structural resemblance is itself evidence
that we are in the presence of myth-making, and myth-making deserves to be
recognised as such.


### Part III: Social Media and the Return of Prayer

#### The Structural Parallel

You do not need to press the analogy hard for it to hold. Consider what prayer is,
structurally: a directed utterance addressed to an absent or diffuse other,
sent into a space where you cannot confirm it has been received, with the hope
of response. The utterance constructs the self--through confession, petition,
praise--as much as it communicates to its recipient. It is performed within
a community of practice whose shared participation validates the act. It follows
rhythms and repetitions--daily prayer, the canonical hours, the rosary--
that structure time and mark the self as belonging to something larger.

Social media posts have exactly this structure. They are directed utterances
addressed to an absent or diffuse other--followers, the platform, "the internet"
-- sent into a space where reception cannot be confirmed in advance. They construct
the self through performance: confession (vulnerability posts), petition
(fundraising, asking for support), praise (sharing what moves us).
They are performed within communities of practice--specific platforms,
specific hashtags, specific discourse norms--whose participation validates
the act. And they follow rhythms and repetitions that structure time:
the morning check, the evening scroll, the compulsive refresh.

The response mechanism of prayer is intermittent and unpredictable--sometimes
the prayer feels answered, sometimes the silence stretches on--and this
intermittent reinforcement is, as the psychology of gambling makes clear,
among the most powerful mechanisms for sustaining behaviour. Social media
engagement operates on exactly the same schedule: the like, the comment,
the viral spread arrive unpredictably, which is why the checking never stops.

#### The Algorithm as Providence

The platform algorithm is, for the social media user, structurally analogous
to divine providence: an intelligence vastly more powerful than the individual,
operating by rules that are partially known and partially inscrutable, rewarding
and punishing in ways that feel both systematic and arbitrary. To go viral is
to receive grace--an amplification of one's utterance that cannot be fully
earned or planned, only hoped for. To be shadowbanned or algorithmically
suppressed is to fall into a kind of invisible disfavour whose cause is difficult
to identify and whose remedy is unclear.

The theologians of the early Church spent considerable energy on the problem of
divine hiddenness: why, if God is good and powerful, does he not make himself
unambiguously present? The content creator's equivalent is: why, given that I
am posting good content and following the rules, is the algorithm not rewarding me?
Both questions circle the same structural feature: a powerful, opaque, consequential
intelligence whose criteria remain partially hidden.

Byung-Chul Han has written extensively, in works such as *In the Swarm* (2017)
and *The Transparency Society* (2015), about the way digital communication has
restructured public life. His argument, broadly, is that the digital public sphere
produces *noise* rather than *discourse*--an endless circulation of self-expression
that generates the sensation of connection without its substance. The crowd in the
digital swarm does not deliberate; it amplifies and attenuates, rewards and punishes,
through mechanisms that feel collective but are in fact coordinated by platform
architectures designed for engagement, not for thought.

What Han does not quite say, but what the prayer parallel illuminates: the
sensation users experience is not merely one of connection. It is one of
*witnessing*--of having one's existence, however briefly, confirmed by something
larger. The desperate quality of social media self-presentation--the hunger
for likes, the anxiety about follower counts, the devastation of being ignored--
is not simply narcissism. It is the specific distress of the person whose prayer
is not being heard.

#### Confession, Community, and Ritual

Durkheim argued that the primary function of religious practice is not
cognitive--it is not about conveying information about the supernatural--
but *social*: it creates and maintains the collective through shared ritual
participation. The act of worship together, of performing the same gestures
and utterances at the same time, produces *collective effervescence*--the
sensation of being part of something larger than the individual self.

The scroll has replaced the pew. The share has replaced the amen. The group
chat has replaced the congregation. None of these are identical to their
predecessors--the differences matter--but the social-psychological
function being served is recognisably the same: the production of belonging,
the inscription of the self into a collective narrative, the regular
re-enactment of shared identity.

The confession box has, similarly, been replaced by the vulnerability post--
the public disclosure of personal struggle, failure, or pain that seeks
communal absolution. What was once spoken in private to a representative of
the sacred has become a public performance seeking the distributed absolution
of strangers. The structure of the act--the disclosure of what is hidden,
the seeking of recognition and forgiveness, the relief of the burden being
lifted--is identical. The audience has changed.

#### The Dopamine Loop and the Dark Night of the Soul

The mystic tradition across multiple religions describes a phenomenon variously
called the *dark night of the soul* (John of the Cross), *acedia* (the desert
fathers), or spiritual desolation (Ignatius of Loyola): a state of emptiness,
dryness, and absence in which the usual consolations of prayer disappear and
the practitioner is left with nothing but the habit of the practice itself.
The tradition is unanimous that this is not a sign of abandonment but a stage
of deepening. The consolations were a scaffold; the building is the practice,
not the feeling.

Social media has its own version: the moment when the like count stops mattering,
when the follower number produces no satisfaction, when the compulsive checking
continues but the relief no longer comes. The platform designers call this
*engagement fatigue*. The mystics would recognise it immediately: it is the
moment when the consolation has been withdrawn and only the compulsion remains.
The significant difference is that the mystical tradition had a framework for
interpreting this state and a practice for navigating it. Social media has only
the recommendation to post more.


### Part IV: The Foreclosure Problem

#### How Totalising Frameworks Close Down Inquiry

The medieval Church's relationship to inquiry was more complex than the
Enlightenment narrative of simple suppression allows. The scholastics--
Aquinas, Duns Scotus, William of Ockham--were rigorous and subtle thinkers
who advanced logic, natural philosophy, and theology simultaneously. The
monasteries preserved and transmitted knowledge that would otherwise have
been lost. Pierre Duhem's work in the early twentieth century argued,
controversially but with serious textual support, that the foundations of
modern science were laid by fourteenth-century Parisian theologians.

But the Church also provided what we might call *premature closure*: a framework
so complete that certain questions became, in practice, not worth pursuing because
the answer was already known. The shape of the cosmos, the age of the world,
the origin of species: these were not questions one approached empirically with
genuine uncertainty, because the framework already specified the answer.
The remarkable thing is not that inquiry was sometimes suppressed--it was--
but that the framework's completeness made suppression largely unnecessary.
Why investigate what you already know?

This is the foreclosure problem: not censorship but *preemptive closure*,
the condition in which the framework is so totalising that questions which fall
outside it become not dangerous but *uninteresting*, not forbidden but
*already answered*.

AI discourse is generating its own preemptive closures. The question of
whether transformative AI will arrive soon, the question of whether existential
risk is the correct frame, the question of whether scaling is the right path:
these are, in the major AI laboratories and in the discourse communities around
them, increasingly being answered in advance. Not because there is evidence
that settles them, but because the framework--AGI is coming, it will be
transformative, safety is the central concern--has become the precondition
for participation in the discourse rather than a conclusion within it.

Feyerabend's complaint about scientific authority--that it had acquired a
dogmatic confidence disproportionate to its epistemic warrant, that it had
become an obstacle to exactly the kind of open inquiry it claimed to embody--
applies with particular force here. The fields most confident about the future
of AI are also the ones with the least track record of successful prediction
about it. The gap between confidence and warrant is a structural feature of
eschatological thinking.

#### What Gets Closed Down

The concrete costs of premature closure in AI discourse are identifiable:

*Near-term harms become secondary.* When the frame is existential risk from
superintelligence, the documented, ongoing harms of current systems--to workers
whose jobs are being displaced, to communities whose data was taken without
consent, to individuals whose faces are misidentified by surveillance systems--
can seem comparatively trivial. Gebru and others have argued explicitly that
the existential risk frame functions, in practice, to deflect attention from
these concrete harms. Whether or not this is intentional, the structural effect
is real.

*Alternative paths become invisible.* If AGI through scaling is the implicit
assumption, then paths that do not go through scaling--embodied learning,
hybrid symbolic-neural architectures, approaches informed by cognitive science--
receive less attention, less funding, and fewer researchers. The Bitter Lesson
(see scaling README) becomes a self-fulfilling prophecy: because we believe
scale will win, we invest in scale, so scale wins, so we believe scale will win.

*The political questions get deferred.* Who controls AI, under what governance
structures, with what accountability, on whose behalf--these are questions
that require political answers. The eschatological frame tends to treat them as
secondary to the technical questions: first we build it safely, then we figure
out the governance. But governance cannot be deferred until after the power
is consolidated, because by then the question of who governs will have been
answered by default.


### Part V: Synthesis--Holding the Question Open

#### Neither Debunking nor Capitulation

The argument of this piece is not that AI is *merely* mythology or that the
concerns of the safety researchers are illusions. It is that AI discourse
has acquired mythological features--quasi-religious structure, eschatological
framing, messianic figures, heresy management, the prayer-like dynamics of
social media amplification--that warrant the same kind of critical awareness
we would bring to other instances of myth-making.

Myths are not simply false. They encode genuine anxieties, respond to real
experiences, and can orient action effectively. The question is not whether
to have frameworks for understanding uncertain territory--frameworks are
unavoidable--but whether to have them consciously or unconsciously, critically
or dogmatically.

Feyerabend's *Against Method* (1975) argued that science's self-presentation as
a uniquely rational, method-governed enterprise was itself a myth--and that
recognising this did not mean abandoning science but approaching it with more
honesty about what it actually is. The same move is available here. Recognising
that AI discourse has quasi-religious features does not mean dismissing it.
It means approaching it with the kind of epistemological awareness that the
discourse itself, at its best, demands.

#### The Specific Value of Historical Consciousness

The historical parallel to Rome's fall and Christianity's rise is useful not
as a prediction--history does not repeat in the simple sense--but as a
reminder that we have been here before. Not in the specific details but in
the general structure: the collapse of a containing framework, the rush of
totalising narrative into the vacuum, the foreclosure of certain questions
by the new orthodoxy, and the long process of recovering the questions that
were foreclosed.

The recovery from medieval closure took centuries and was partial. The
Reformation opened some questions while closing others. The Enlightenment
opened some questions while closing others. We are still, arguably, living
inside the closures that earlier epistemic revolutions created and did not
notice.

The invitation of historical consciousness is not pessimism but humility:
the recognition that we are probably closing down some questions that future
generations will need to reopen, and that this is not a reason to stop thinking
but a reason to think as carefully as possible about what we are assuming in advance.

#### What Remains After the Demythologising

Strip away the eschatological framing, the messianic figures, the heresy
management, and the prayer-like dynamics of the discourse, and what remains?

A set of genuinely hard technical problems--in safety, in alignment, in
interpretability--that require sustained, careful, well-funded research.
A set of genuinely hard political problems--in governance, in accountability,
in the distribution of benefits and harms--that require sustained, careful,
democratic engagement. And a set of genuinely hard philosophical problems--
about consciousness, about the nature of values, about what we owe to systems
we build--that require sustained, careful inquiry without premature closure.

None of these problems is made easier by the mythological framing. Some are made
harder: the eschatological urgency tends to compress the time horizons within
which careful work can be done, to reward dramatic claims over careful ones,
and to punish the kind of honest uncertainty that genuine difficulty requires.

The history of thought offers a limited but real consolation: the questions
that get foreclosed by one epistemic regime tend to survive, in marginal and
subterranean forms, until the conditions for their recovery arrive. The work
done here--and in the wider tradition of careful, critical, philosophically
serious engagement with AI--is part of that survival. Not dramatic, not
eschatological, not going viral. But present, and waiting.
