
## A Very Short History of 3D Development

he historical development of 3D graphics spans several decades, evolving from basic geometric representations to sophisticated, photorealistic rendering techniques. It began in the 1960s and 1970s with foundational concepts like the vertex, edge, and face, which formed the building blocks of early wireframe models. Pioneers such as Ivan Sutherland's Sketchpad system in 1963 introduced interactive manipulation of vertices and edges, allowing users to define simple 3D shapes in Euclidean space. These early systems rendered only outlines, without filled surfaces, as computing power was limited.

By the mid-1970s, the polygon emerged as a key primitive, enabling the creation of solid meshes composed of connected faces—typically triangles or quadrilaterals—for more complete object representations. Researchers like Henri Gouraud developed Gouraud shading in 1971, which interpolated color across polygon faces based on vertex attributes, providing smoother gradients than flat shading and reducing the faceted appearance of low-polygon meshes. This was a significant step toward realistic illumination, relying on normal vectors at vertices to compute lighting.

The 1980s saw advancements in shading models, with Bui Tuong Phong introducing Phong shading in 1973 (though widely adopted later), which interpolated normal vectors across faces for per-fragment lighting calculations, yielding more accurate specular highlights and surface details. Rasterisation became central to the rendering pipeline, converting vector-based meshes into pixel grids on screens. Techniques like z-buffering, proposed by Edwin Catmull in 1974, solved visibility issues by storing depth values per pixel, ensuring correct occlusion of overlapping objects. Back-face culling also emerged as an optimization, discarding polygons facing away from the camera to reduce computational load.

Texture mapping, invented by Catmull in his 1974 PhD thesis, revolutionized surface detail by applying 2D images to 3D meshes via UV mapping, allowing realistic patterns without increasing polygon counts. This era also introduced the model-view-projection (MVP) matrix for transforming vertices from local model space through view and projection spaces to the screen, using homogeneous coordinates and affine transformations to handle translation, rotation, and scaling efficiently. Orthographic projection was common for technical applications, while perspective projection mimicked human vision for immersive scenes.

In the 1990s, hardware acceleration transformed the field. Graphics processing units (GPUs) began handling fixed-function pipelines, including vertex processing and fragment shading. Bump maps added illusory surface detail by perturbing normals, evolving into normal maps for more precise bump simulation. The framebuffer expanded to include depth and stencil buffers, enabling advanced effects like alpha blending for transparency. Games like Doom (1993) popularized real-time rasterisation with simple meshes, while Quake (1996) incorporated ambient occlusion approximations and early global illumination ideas.

The early 2000s brought programmable shaders, with APIs like OpenGL and DirectX allowing custom vertex shaders and fragment shaders. This enabled techniques like deferred shading, separating geometry and lighting passes for efficiency in complex scenes with multiple lights. Tessellation shaders allowed dynamic mesh subdivision, increasing detail adaptively. Level of detail (LOD) systems optimized distant objects with simplified meshes, and frustum culling, often using bounding boxes or octrees, prevented rendering of off-screen geometry. Mipmaps and texture filtering (bilinear, trilinear, and anisotropic) reduced aliasing in scaled textures.

By the 2010s, physically based rendering (PBR) became standard, simulating realistic light-material interactions using properties like roughness and metallicness, often combined with normal maps and shader graphs for artist-friendly material creation. Ray tracing, long used in offline rendering (e.g., via render farms for films), went real-time with hardware support in GPUs like NVIDIA's RTX series in 2018, enabling accurate global illumination, reflections, and shadows. Compute shaders extended GPUs beyond graphics for simulations, while signed distance fields (SDFs) facilitated procedural geometry via ray marching.

Today, in the mi 2020s, 3D graphics integrate AI-driven upscaling, voxel-based volumes for destructible environments, and advanced skinning/rigging for hyper-realistic animations. Instancing and vertex buffer objects (VBOs) with index buffer objects (IBOs) optimize draw calls for massive scenes, while occlusion culling and render queues manage complex render pipelines. From humble vertices to immersive virtual worlds, these developments have made 3D graphics indispensable in gaming, film, and simulation.
